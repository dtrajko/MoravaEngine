MoravaEngine · a game on the engine

Shooter

A feasibility study and implementation plan for a single-player shooter. The player switches between a first-person and a third-person view at any time. Missions are set in a large, closed military and industrial base with several floors, catwalks and plenty of places to hide. The weapons are an AK-style rifle (semi-automatic and automatic), a pistol and a knife. There are health packs and armor, and guards with simple AI that patrol, hear, see, raise the alarm and fight. The inspiration is how Counter-Strike 1.6 and CS2, GoldenEye 007 (1997), Doom and Quake play: their maps, cover and verticality. Their content is not copied: the levels, models, names and sounds are our own or CC0. Multiplayer is kept in view: it's not built now, but nothing here rules it out.

Drafted November 15, 2026, while the racing game's Phase 11 is finishing. It is a plan, not agreed work: it waits for the user's review. Names that exist in the code are written as they are. Names of new classes, files and settings are proposals. Once building starts, this page is kept up to date with "As built", "Checked" and "Not done" boxes, as the Racing plan is.

The verdict

Feasible on this engine, with about nine pieces of engine work first or alongside
  • About two thirds of what a shooter needs is already there. The racing game, the Sample project (Erika's third-person character and camera, the collector milestone) and the engine phases built a lot of it:
    • Jolt physics: the character controller, raycasts, sphere casts and overlap queries with layers, and triggers.
    • C# scripting, prefabs (spawned and destroyed while the game runs), and an animator with states, crossfades and animation events.
    • 3D audio, game UI with menus and settings, and save data.
    • GPU particles, trails, ray-traced global illumination, quality tiers, and the game build.
  • What is missing is what makes it a shooter, all of it standard and well understood:
    • Navigation for the AI (a navmesh, from Recast/Detour).
    • A first-person view model: the arms and gun drawn on top, never through walls.
    • Bullet-hole decals.
    • Entities that follow a bone (a gun in a hand, a hitbox on a head).
    • Aiming the upper body while the legs walk (an animation layer).
    • Lights driven from C# (muzzle flashes, alarm lights).
    • Crouching (the character's capsule changing height).
    They are described in E1–E9; each is one engine phase, like the racing game's particles and trails were.
  • The biggest risk is content, not code: rigged, animated characters holding guns, under CC0. Phase 0 checks the candidate packs before anything else is built.
  • Multiplayer is a separate, large project (prediction, lag compensation, a server). The single-player game is built so that it can come later without a rewrite (the rules), but none of it is in this plan's phases.

The plan has eleven phases (0–10). The first playable result, a player walking and shooting in a greybox level, comes after Phase 2. Guards that fight back come in Phase 5. The first full mission is Phase 7.

The decisions

The user's (2026-11-15)

  • Single player now; multiplayer in perspective.
  • Inspiration: Counter-Strike 1.6 and CS2, GoldenEye 007 (N64), Doom and Quake. That means large, closed play areas with lots of room to hide and several levels (floors).
  • The usual things: basic weapons (an AK-47-style rifle, semi-automatic), health packs, and NPCs with basic AI.
  • First person and third person, with a switch between them at any time.
  • Stylized low-poly, like the Racing game.
  • The first level: a military and industrial base.
  • Missions with objectives (GoldenEye-style): reach places, find things, deal with guards, get out.
  • CC0 assets only, or our own (scripts, Blender), as in Racing.

Proposed

  • The project is Projects/Outpost: "Outpost" is a working title, to be changed at will.
  • Hitscan weapons: a shot is a ray, hitting at once, as in CS. Grenades and rockets, if they ever come, are projectiles (physics bodies).
  • Generic weapon names ("assault rifle", "pistol"), modeled on the AK's look and behavior but not named after a real product. The rifle switches between semi-automatic and automatic fire.
  • The level is built by a script in Blender, as the racing tracks are: Tools/LevelBuilder reads a data file describing buildings, floors, rooms, stairs and catwalks, and writes the models, the colliders, and the spots for spawning, cover and patrols. Props (crates, barrels, containers) come from CC0 kits.
  • The guards' AI is written in C# on top of an engine navmesh. It is a state machine (patrol, suspicious, alert, combat, search), with sight and hearing, an alarm, and difficulty settings (Easy, Normal, Hard).
  • Checkpoints save the mission's progress. Dying restarts from the last one.
  • Mouse and keyboard first, with a gamepad supported (the input actions already handle both).

Risks

Content: animated characters with guns, under CC0 The game needs a player body and guards with idle, walk, run, crouch, aim, fire, reload, hit and death animations, holding a rifle and a pistol. CC0 kits with such sets exist in the stylized low-poly style (Quaternius's character and shooter packs, Kenney's characters and blaster kit are the first candidates). Phase 0 checks each one's license, skeleton, animation list and look before anything depends on them. Fallbacks: retarget CC0 animations onto one CC0 skeleton in Blender by script, or keep guards simpler (fewer states) in the first mission.
Many lights indoors Shadow maps exist for 4 spot and 4 point lights at once. A base with many lamps keeps most of them unshadowed (the probes give the bounce light), shadows only for a few near the player, chosen each frame. This is measured in Phase 6.
Drawing what's behind walls The renderer culls by the camera's view (frustum culling), not by what walls hide. A multi-floor base draws every room in front of the camera, even the ones behind walls. The level builder splits the level into chunks per room and floor (as the racing track is in chunks), and the budget is measured in Phase 6. If it isn't met, occlusion culling (portals between rooms, or the depth prepass's results) becomes engine work.
The AI's cost 15 to 25 guards per level, each thinking a few times a second (not every frame), with sight rays spread over frames. Pathfinding on a navmesh is cheap. The risk is small but measured in Phase 5.
The third-person aim The camera sits behind the shoulder, the gun in front of the body: the camera may see a target the gun can't (around a corner). The shot is checked from the muzzle too (first and third person).

The performance target

The same as Racing, on the user's machine (RTX 3070 Ti, Ryzen 9 5900X, 2560×1440 at 180 Hz):

  • Medium (the default, no ray tracing) aims at 120 fps and is never under 60, counted on the 1% lows: in a firefight, with smoke and muzzle flashes, and the whole level's guards alive.
  • High and Ultra (ray tracing): never under 60.

Measured with the test player (how each phase is checked) and --frame-stats, in Phases 5, 6 and 9.

The game's design

The player

  • Moving: running at about 5.5 m/s, walking quietly at 2.5 (guards hear less), crouching at 2. Jumping about 0.6 m (onto crates, over railings). No sprint at first: in CS's tradition, speed depends on the weapon held (the knife is fastest).
  • Health 100, armor up to 100: armor takes two thirds of the damage while it lasts. No health regeneration: health comes from health packs (small +25, large +50) and armor from vests.
  • Falling: damage from about 5 m.

The weapons

WeaponFireDamage (body, head)Magazine, spareNotes
Assault rifle (AK-style)Semi or automatic, 600 shots/min30, ×4 to the head30, 90Recoil climbs in a set pattern then drifts; accurate when still, less when moving or jumping
PistolSemi20, ×412, 48Fast to draw; the fallback
KnifeSlash50 (from behind: a takedown)–Silent; fastest to run with
  • Each weapon's numbers are a data file in the project (Weapons/*.weapon.yaml), not code: tuning without rebuilding, and the same files a server would read later.
  • Aiming down the sights narrows the spread and the view, and slows the player.
  • Ammunition is picked up from boxes and from guards' dropped guns.
  • Hits: bullets stop at walls. Thin materials (wooden crates, sheet metal) can be marked as ones that bullets pass through with reduced damage (later, if the levels ask for it).
  • Feedback: muzzle flash (particles, a short light: E8), tracer, impact sparks or dust by material, a bullet hole (E6), a hit marker on the crosshair and a sound when a guard is hit, camera kick.

A mission

  • A briefing screen with the objectives, the level, then a debriefing with the time, accuracy, guards defeated, alarms raised and a rating.
  • Objectives: reach a place, pick up an item (documents, a key card), use a switch (cut the power, open a gate), destroy something (a radio), defeat a named guard, leave by the exit. Some optional, some only on harder difficulties (as in GoldenEye).
  • The alarm: a guard who sees the player may run to an alarm panel. If it sounds, more guards come and the doors to some areas lock. Stealth (the knife, walking quietly, guards that haven't seen you) is rewarded, but not required.
  • Difficulty changes the guards' accuracy, reaction time, damage and numbers, and the objectives.

First and third person

V switches at any time, also while moving or shooting; the choice is kept in the settings. Both views play by the same rules: movement, the hit test and the weapons don't change, only the camera and what is drawn.

Aim and hit testThe shot goes from the camera through the crosshair to find the aim point; then from the muzzle to that point. If something is in between (a corner the camera sees past), that is what it hits. In first person the two rays nearly match.
Aiming down the sightsThe sights come up to the eye, the view narrowsThe camera moves in closer over the shoulder, the view narrows

The player is one character for both views: one CharacterController, one body model with its animator, and a view-model child shown only in first person. Guards see, hear and hit the same body in both views, so the view is a presentation choice with no effect on gameplay. That also matters for multiplayer later.

The guards' AI

In C# (GuardBrain), over the navmesh (E1). Each guard thinks 5–10 times a second, at different moments (a fraction of the guards each frame). Movement and animation run every frame.

Idle, patrolStands at a post or walks a route of the level's patrol points; looks aroundIt notices something (suspicion grows)
SuspiciousTurns to look, walks to where the noise or the glimpse wasSure: combat. Nothing found: back to patrol after a while

  • Sight: a cone (about 110°, 40 m by day, shorter in the dark and when the player crouches or stands still), and rays to the player's head and chest; suspicion builds over time instead of an instant alert.
  • Hearing: gunshots (far), footsteps when running (near), bodies falling, bullets hitting nearby. Sounds are events with a position and a loudness, sent to the guards in range: a "noise" C# event, not the audio system.
  • Aim: guards miss on purpose at first and get better the longer they see the player. Their accuracy also depends on distance, movement and difficulty.
  • Cover: the level builder writes cover spots (behind low walls, crates, door frames) with the directions they protect from. A guard rates the nearby spots by safety and line of sight.
  • Debug views (kept, as the user likes): each guard's state, its sight cone, its path, the noises it heard, and the cover spots rated.

The level and how it is made

The first level: the base

About 150 × 120 m inside a fence and cliffs. Seen from above:

  • A warehouse (two floors high): racks of crates, catwalks around the walls, an office on the upper level, a loading bay and a freight lift.
  • An office building (three floors and a roof): a stairwell, corridors, rooms, windows overlooking the yard.
  • A basement and bunker under the office: a server room, a generator, a tunnel to the warehouse.
  • The yard: shipping containers (stacked two high), trucks, sandbags, two guard towers, the gate.
  • Several routes between any two places (a stair, a ladder later, a catwalk, a tunnel), long and short sight lines, and dark corners: room to hide and to flank.

This is our own layout, not a remake of any existing map.

How it is made

  • Tools/LevelBuilder/build_level.py runs in Blender without its window, as the track builder does. It reads Levels/Base.level.json: buildings as boxes with floors, rooms on a grid (walls, doors, windows), stairs, ramps, catwalks, openings, plus where the props, lights, patrol points, cover, pickups and objectives go.
  • It writes chunks per building and floor (Level_Base_Warehouse_F1.glb and so on): the drawn meshes, simpler collider meshes, and a Base.spots.json with the spawns, patrol routes, cover spots, pickups and objectives that the scene reads.
  • Greybox first: plain colored surfaces, to play the layout before it is dressed. The same data later gets the materials (concrete, metal, painted walls) and the CC0 props.
  • Lighting: the sun outside. Inside, lamps as point and spot lights (most without shadows, see the risks), and a probe volume per building for the bounce light.
  • The navmesh is baked from the colliders (E1), in the editor, and saved with the scene.

Keeping multiplayer possible

Online multiplayer for a shooter means a server that decides (authoritative), clients that predict their own movement and correct it, other players drawn slightly in the past and smoothed, and hit tests that rewind the targets to what the shooter saw (lag compensation). That is a project the size of this whole plan, with its own engine work (networking, replication), and not part of it. What the single-player game does now so that it can come later:

  • Input as commands: the player's actions each fixed step become a small struct (move, look, fire, crouch...), and the player character is driven only from those. A remote player or a bot sends the same commands.
  • The game's rules in the fixed step (movement, shots, damage, pickups), and presentation (camera, animation, sounds, effects) in the frame update, reacting to events.
  • One way to deal damage (Damage.Apply(target, amount, source, hit)), with the event telling the rest of the game.
  • No "the player" singletons in game code: guards look for the nearest visible "actor" with a team, so a second human player is just another actor.
  • Weapons and characters as data, the same numbers on every machine.
  • Random numbers seeded per shot (spread, recoil drift), so a server and a client agree.

The engine work (E1–E9)

Each is an engine feature for any game, written up in the engine guide (likely a new section, as 12.1 Particles was) with its Inspector, C# API, overlay or debug view, and a test scene. They are done inside the shooter's phases, as the racing game's particles were.

E1 Navigation

It can come first, on its own The user sees AI navigation as one of the major quality improvements for MoravaEngine (2026-11-15). It is the largest piece here, useful to any game (the Sample project's characters, companions, crowds), and it doesn't depend on the shooter's content. So it can be built as an engine phase before the shooter starts (or alongside its Phase 0), tested on existing scenes: Sample's levels and a greybox with floors and stairs from the level builder. Phase 4 then only applies it to the shooter's levels.
  • Recast and Detour (zlib license) as a submodule, built on all three CI platforms.
  • A Nav Mesh component on a scene entity: agent radius, height, step, slope; Bake in the editor from the static colliders (and walkable areas marked), saved as a cooked file next to the scene; drawn in a debug view.
  • C#: Navigation.FindPath(from, to), Navigation.Raycast (a walkable straight line?), the nearest point on the mesh; a Nav Agent component that follows a path with the character controller, with local avoidance between agents (Detour's crowd).
  • Doors and lifts: links between separate parts of the mesh, which can be turned off (a locked door).

E2 The first-person view model

  • Entities marked View Model are drawn after the scene with their own field of view and a near depth range, so they never cut into a wall the player stands against. They are lit by the same lights and probes. They cast no shadows (the body does).
  • Kept out of TAA's smearing (they move with the camera) and out of the ray-traced passes.

E3 Bone attachments, E4 Hitboxes

  • A Bone Attachment component: the entity follows a named bone of an animated model (with an offset). Guns in hands, a helmet, a flashlight, hitboxes.
  • Hitboxes: capsules and boxes on bones, on a Hitbox physics layer: kinematic, moved to the bones each step, only hit by shot rays. A hit tells which part (head, chest, limb) for damage multipliers.
  • C#: Animator.GetBoneTransform(name), for the muzzle in third person and for effects.

E5 Animation layers and aim offset

  • The animator gets layers: a second state machine for the upper body (a bone mask from the spine up) on top of the legs' locomotion, with a weight.
  • An aim offset: a few poses (aim up, center, down; left, right) blended by two parameters, so the upper body points the gun where the camera or the guard looks.
  • A 1D blend for locomotion (idle, walk, run by speed) instead of crossfades only. Blend trees in general stay in "Later" unless needed.

E6 Decals

  • A Decal component: a box that projects a texture onto the opaque surfaces inside it (bullet holes, scorch marks, posters, stains), drawn after the opaque pass from the depth and normals, multiplied (as the trails are) or blended.
  • C#: Decals.Spawn(texture, position, normal, size, lifetime) into a pool with a limit (the oldest fade out), not as entities, for the hundreds of bullet holes in a firefight.

E7 Crouch, E8 Lights in C#, E9 Sound in rooms

  • E7: CharacterController.Height can change while the game runs, refused (stays crouched) when something is above.
  • E8: PointLight and SpotLight in C#: color, intensity, range, on/off, shadows; short-lived flash lights from a pool.
  • E9: sounds behind walls muffled (a ray from listener to source; a low-pass filter and less volume), and reverb zones (a hall, a corridor, outdoors) as trigger volumes. Optional: the game works without it.

The phases

Phase 0: the project, and the content check

  • Projects/Outpost with its input actions, a greybox test scene, and the license notes.
  • The candidate CC0 character, gun and animation packs: imported, checked for license, skeleton, animations (the list in risks) and look; one shared skeleton chosen. A go/no-go for the content.
  • Tools/LevelBuilder version 1: rooms, floors, stairs and walls from a data file, greybox.

Phase 1: the player in both views

  • First-person movement: walk, run, walk quietly, jump, fall damage, footsteps; crouch (E7).
  • The body model and animator; the third-person camera (from Sample's, over the shoulder, with collision); the V switch.
  • The view model (E2) with an empty-handed or placeholder gun.
  • Input as commands from the start (the rules).

Phase 2: weapons

  • Weapon data files; the rifle (semi and automatic), the pistol, the knife; switching, reloading, ammo.
  • The hit test in both views (camera ray, then muzzle ray); recoil, spread, aiming down the sights.
  • Effects: muzzle flash (particles and a light: E8), tracers, impacts by material, bullet holes (E6); the guns' sounds; the gun in the body's hands (E3) and the upper body aiming (E5).
  • Damage, and targets to shoot (dummies) in the greybox.

Phase 3: health, pickups and the HUD

  • Health and armor, health packs, vests, ammo boxes; death and restart.
  • The HUD: crosshair (it spreads with the weapon's spread), health, armor, ammo, the weapon, a hit marker, the direction damage came from, objectives; menus and settings (from Racing's), the view and mouse sensitivity.

Phase 4: navigation (E1)

  • Recast/Detour in the engine, baking, the debug view, the C# API, agents; on the greybox level across floors.
  • If E1 was built ahead as its own engine phase (the note in E1), this phase is only the bake and the door links for the shooter's levels.

Phase 5: the guards

  • The guard prefab: model, animator with layers (E5), gun in hand (E3), hitboxes (E4).
  • GuardBrain: the states, sight, hearing, aim, cover, groups, the alarm; debug views.
  • Difficulty settings; the budget with 25 guards.

Phase 6: the base

  • The level builder in full; the base's buildings, floors, basement and yard; first in greybox, played and changed, then dressed with materials, props, lights and probes.
  • Doors (opened with Use, some locked), the freight lift, the navmesh with door links.
  • The lights and drawing budget (risks): chunking, shadowed lights, occlusion if needed.

Phase 7: the mission

  • Objectives, triggers, the alarm, checkpoints and saves, the briefing and debriefing, the rating, the main menu.

Phase 8: feel and sound

  • Sounds by our scripts and CC0; music; sound occlusion and reverb (E9) if there's time; camera shake, hit feedback, death effects; tuning with the test player and by playing.

Phase 9: the budget and the game build

  • Medium at 120 fps in the heaviest firefight; the game built and run as the player would.

Phase 10: a second mission

  • A second level from the same builder (a night version of the base, or a new area), to prove the tools make levels quickly.

Later

  • More weapons: a shotgun, a sniper rifle with a scope, grenades (projectiles, explosions with physics)
  • Ragdolls (Jolt has them) for deaths; bullets that knock props about
  • Ladders, vents to crawl through, leaning around corners
  • A silenced pistol and fuller stealth (bodies found, lights shot out)
  • Co-op and versus multiplayer: networking in the engine, and the work in keeping it possible
  • Bots for CS-style rounds on the same maps (the guards' AI, on teams)

How each phase is checked

  • A test player: a script that drives the player through the level by commands, as the racing test driver drives the car: walks a route of points, shoots at targets, switches views and weapons. It runs unattended with --exit-after, in the editor and the built player, and in Debug once a phase (asserts).
  • Guards against the test player: fights logged (who saw whom, shots, hits, deaths), the guards' debug views captured.
  • Frame stats (--frame-stats) in the heaviest scenes; no validation messages, no script problems.
  • The user plays it in the editor's Play: the views, the feel and the fun are judged there.
