Refraction: no separate scene pass. The water reuses what the main pass has already drawn. The main scene render pass is split into two Vulkan render passes over the same framebuffer:

1. Main pass: clears, then draws the sky and the opaque models.
2. Copy, outside any render pass: color and depth go into the water's two textures.
3. Continue pass: loads the same attachments instead of clearing them, then draws the water and the grid.

The copy and the continue pass only happen while water exists; without water there's no split. The scene geometry is drawn once.

Reflection: none yet; Phase 3 adds one. Today the water reflects only the environment map, which costs nothing extra. Phase 3 adds a separate pass that draws the scene again from the mirrored camera into its own half-resolution image. That pass is the cost you agreed was worth it.

The reflection pass can't reuse the main pass like refraction does. A reflection shows the scene from a different viewpoint, from below the water plane looking up, and that view isn't in the main image. A screen-space shortcut would miss anything off-screen, which is why you chose planar reflection.

Here's the plan. I'd build Vulkan water as a new design that keeps the OpenGL version's good ideas, not as a line-by-line port. Underneath is what I'd do differently from OpenGL and why, followed by three questions.

Phases

Each phase ends with something you can see and test.

Phase 1 – A water surface you can see, with no extra passes
- Water object: a rectangle you add from a new Water panel, with Add/Remove, height, size, colors and wave settings. The gizmo moves it, and it can be selected like a model. For this scene, IsWaterOnScene() becomes "a water object exists", so it fits the check from today's fix.
- Water shader, drawn after the opaque models into the HDR scene image:
  - Two scrolling normal maps for the waves, using the existing waterNormal.png and waterDuDv.png.
  - Reflections from the environment map (the same blurred sky the PBR shaders use), blended by a Fresnel term.
  - A sun highlight from the directional light, shadowed by the existing sun shadow maps.
- At this point it already looks like water, at almost no cost.

Phase 2 – Refraction without re-drawing the scene
- After the opaque models are drawn, copy the scene's color and depth into textures the water shader can read. This replaces OpenGL's second full scene render with a clip plane. Unity, Unreal and Godot all do it this way.
- The water shader uses that copy for:
  - Distorted refraction, skipping samples that land on objects in front of the water, so they don't smear into it.
  - Depth-based color: the color depends on how much water the light passes through (Beer–Lambert absorption), so shallow water is clear and deep water is dark and tinted.
  - Soft edges and foam where the water meets the shore or objects.

Phase 3 – Planar reflection
- A reflection image at half or quarter resolution, re-rendering the scene from a mirrored camera.
- Mirrored camera: built from a true reflection matrix, so it works with any camera, including roll, and leaves the camera controller untouched.
- Clipping: an oblique projection clips at the water plane, so the PBR shaders need no clip-plane changes.
- Camera data for this pass: a second set of per-frame camera data. Today's single camera buffer can't hold two views in one frame. This also prepares for later reflection probes.
- Pipeline compatibility: the reflection image uses the same formats as the scene image so the existing pipelines can draw into it. Variants with reversed winding are needed, because a mirror flips triangle winding.
- Falls back to the environment map where the reflection image has no data (screen edges, Phase 3 turned off).

Phase 4 – Underwater
- Fog and absorption applied once in the composite pass from the scene depth, covering every object and the sky. OpenGL tints each object in its own shader instead.
- The water surface seen from below shows the sky only within a cone overhead (Snell's window), and mirrors the underwater scene outside it (total internal reflection).
- Handles the waterline when the camera crosses the surface.

Phase 5 – Optional
Waves in the geometry itself (Gerstner waves on a grid), caustics on underwater surfaces, flow maps.

Concerns

- Cost: planar reflection draws the scene a second time each frame. Half resolution plus an on/off switch keeps it manageable, and the shadow maps are reused.
- Scene image format: the scene renders to RGBA32F, which makes the per-frame copy and the reflection image heavy. RGBA16F is plenty for HDR. Switching is a small separate change, and I'd do it first.
- Saving: like models, the water object won't be saved until scene save/load exists.

Questions

1. Number of water planes: is one per scene enough for now? Each plane at a different height needs its own reflection render. My recommendation is one now, more later.
2. Reflection method: planar reflection (accurate, costs a scene pass) or screen-space reflection (cheap, but misses anything off-screen)? I recommend planar, with the environment map as fallback.
3. Geometric waves (Phase 5): in scope now, or after the rest? I suggest after: normal maps look convincing for calm water.

If the plan works for you, I'd start with Phase 1, after switching the scene image to RGBA16F. I can also publish the plan as a page if you want to keep it.
